How to use this guide

The report's central story

One spatially randomized simulation environment — designed and implemented entirely by this author — generates data for two connected learning problems: surgical-instrument object detection and Diffusion Policy manipulation. A scripted finite state machine links the learned components into a safer, modular pick-and-place application.

Central research question

  • Can a shared randomized simulation support both instrument detection and Diffusion Policy manipulation, allowing a Franka robot to reliably pick thin surgical instruments with varied geometries and place them into a tray?

Recommended narrative

  1. Thin surgical instruments are difficult to grasp: small contact surfaces, high tipping risk, limited table clearance, and instrument-specific balance points that defeat fixed scripted trajectories.
  2. A shared Isaac Lab environment (ScissorGraspEnv, scripted FSM, and HDF5 recorder — all original contributions by this author) creates varied but controlled scenes with full reproducibility.
  3. The environment produces synthetic detection annotations (RGB + per-instance segmentation masks) and robot demonstrations recorded via the scripted FSM expert.
  4. RF-DETR-Seg is trained on the synthetic data to select and localize the target instrument; the scripted FSM handles predictable global approach and transport.
  5. Diffusion Policy is trained on FSM expert demonstrations and handles the short, contact-rich, visually sensitive pick-lift phase where scripted motion would be brittle.
  6. Each component is verified independently before evaluating the complete pick-and-place application end-to-end.

Section 02

Introduction: Background, Motivation, Objectives, and Scope

2.1 Background

Introduce robotic manipulation, visual perception, and learning from demonstrations. Explain why thin surgical instruments require both precise scene understanding (detection) and highly repeatable contact-rich local interaction (Diffusion Policy) that exceed what scripted motion can reliably deliver.

2.2 Motivation

Explain why the project is worth doing:

  • Thin, flat, curved, or scissor-like geometries are harder to grasp than standard blocks — contact area can be less than a millimetre wide.
  • Insufficient table clearance causes gripper-finger collisions or weak, off-centre contact during descent.
  • An off-centre grasp makes an instrument rotate, slip, or tip immediately after lift.
  • Fully scripted grasping is brittle under position, yaw, and instrument-geometry variation.
  • Diffusion Policy handles multimodal action distributions near contact, where deterministic policies converge to averaging failures.
  • Data-contract errors in HDF5 recordings must be caught before expensive large-scale collection and training begin.

2.3 Objectives

  • Create a reusable simulated hospital manipulation environment (ScissorGraspEnv in Isaac Lab).
  • Represent several surgical-instrument geometries and their selected grasp regions in USD assets.
  • Randomize object locations and orientations while maintaining reachability and stability.
  • Generate synthetic RGB + instance-mask data for RF-DETR-Seg training and evaluation.
  • Record scripted FSM expert demonstrations in HDF5 format and convert to a robomimic-compatible dataset.
  • Train and evaluate a Diffusion Policy for the local pick-lift manipulation skill.
  • Integrate RF-DETR-Seg, scripted FSM, and Diffusion Policy into a complete pick-and-place application.
  • Evaluate performance across instruments, scene layouts, and starting-pose perturbations.

2.4 Scope and assumptions

  • The robot picks and places instruments; it does not perform medical procedures.
  • The current work is simulation-based and uses a fixed-height table.
  • Initial scenes contain reachable, stable, non-overlapping instruments.
  • Predictable global motion remains scripted via FSM; the contact-rich pick-lift phase is learned via Diffusion Policy.

Problem Definition and Overall System Design

4.1 Formal task definition

Given a requested instrument in a spatially randomized scene, detect and localize it with RF-DETR-Seg, approach a feasible grasp region via scripted FSM, execute a Diffusion Policy pick-lift, validate lift success, transport the instrument to the tray via scripted FSM, and place it stably.

4.3 Requirements and success criteria

  • RF-DETR-Seg correctly identifies the target instrument and provides a valid instance mask.
  • Grasp approach position derived from the detection mask is reachable and collision-free.
  • Objects are stable and sufficiently separated after physics settling.
  • Camera, state, action, and timestamp data remain synchronized in every recorded episode.
  • A successful pick-lift reaches the required height and holds the instrument without invalid contact for the required duration.
  • A successful placement releases the instrument inside the tray and leaves it stable after gripper retreat.

5.1 Complete application pipeline

RF-DETR-Seg detect target → Scripted FSM approach → Diffusion Policy pick_lift → Lift check →
Scripted FSM transport → FSM / DP place → Place check → Repeat

5.2 Division of responsibility

  • RF-DETR-Seg selects the target instrument and estimates the scene position for the scripted approach.
  • Scripted FSM handles predictable global motion (approach, transport) and enforces known kinematic constraints.
  • Diffusion Policy handles the short, visually sensitive, contact-rich pick-lift phase where scripted motion would fail.
  • Success gates (height check, force check, tray containment) stop failures from propagating into later stages.

Research Method: Environment and Randomization

Authorship note — shared infrastructure: ScissorGraspEnv, the scripted FSM, and the HDF5 recorder described in §6.1–6.8 are original contributions designed and implemented solely by Angel. Jordan's OpenVLA track uses the same shared scene-generation API and observation schema without modifying these components.

6.1 Simulation setup

Describe the hospital scene, Franka robot, operating table, instrument tray, instrument USD assets, front camera, wrist camera, coordinate frames, physics timestep, control frequency, and workspace boundaries defined in ScissorGraspEnv (Isaac Lab).

6.2 Scene generation

  1. Select the target instrument class and any distractor instruments for the scene.
  2. Sample target and distractor XY positions and yaw angles within the allowed workspace grid.
  3. Check minimum separation, grasp reachability, and workspace boundary constraints.
  4. Spawn the objects and allow the physics engine to settle for the required number of steps.
  5. Reject scenes with overlapping objects or unstable final poses; resample until an accepted scene is found.
  6. Save the accepted random seed and full scene metadata for reproducibility.

6.3 Why randomization matters

  • Prevents detection and policy training from depending on a single fixed layout.
  • Tests generalization to varied instrument positions and yaw angles.
  • Produces diverse backgrounds and spatial relationships for RF-DETR-Seg training.
  • Allows held-out scene configurations to be generated systematically for evaluation.

6.4 Evidence to include

  • Top-down image of the allowed workspace and target-placement grid.
  • Examples of at least three accepted randomized scenes across different instruments.
  • Examples of rejected overlap, reachability, and stability failure cases.
  • Histogram or coverage plot of sampled XY positions and yaw angles.
  • Table of held-out scene seeds and their evaluation conditions.

6.10 Verification before large-scale collection

  • Images decode at the expected resolution and per-episode frame count.
  • State and action vector dimensions match the Diffusion Policy model configuration file.
  • All values are finite and episode timestamps are strictly ordered.
  • Episode lengths are consistent across image, state, and action arrays.
  • The robomimic data loader reads sample episodes and computes normalization statistics without errors.
  • Visual inspection of rendered rollout videos confirms that recorded actions correspond to the displayed motion.

7.2 Runtime integration

Explain that Isaac Lab (simulation) and Diffusion Policy inference run in separate Python environments. At each control step the simulator sends the stacked observation window (front RGB, wrist RGB, robot state) to the Diffusion Policy inference server, which returns an action chunk. Before execution the runtime controller validates that all values are finite, reference frames are correct, rotation quaternions are unit-normalised, and action magnitudes are within defined workspace limits.

Reporting rule: successful dataset loading or client–server communication proves interface compatibility; it does not prove successful object manipulation. Report manipulation success rates only from actual execution trials.

8.2 Failure taxonomy

  • Wrong instrument class detected or detection confidence below the operating threshold.
  • Grasp approach position derived from the mask is unreachable or causes a collision.
  • Insufficient table clearance during gripper descent phase.
  • Off-centre Diffusion Policy action: instrument rotates, slips, or tips during lift.
  • Instrument dropped during scripted transport phase (out-of-grasp trajectory disturbance).
  • Placement outside tray boundary or instrument unstable after gripper opens.
  • Invalid Diffusion Policy action chunk: NaN values, workspace limit violation, or inference timeout.

For each failure category, present frequency across trials, likely root cause, and a proposed remediation.

Section 10

Limitations, Future Work, Conclusion, and References

10.1 Limitations

  • Simulation-only results do not yet demonstrate real-world Franka performance.
  • The instrument set and scene conditions evaluated are limited in number and variety.
  • Lighting, texture, physics, and camera differences may create a sim-to-real gap for both RF-DETR-Seg and Diffusion Policy.
  • Diffusion Policy training and statistically meaningful task evaluation may still be incomplete at the time of submission.
  • Detection-to-manipulation integration may require further closed-loop testing before reporting complete-cycle results.

10.2 Future work

  • Collect a larger, class-balanced demonstration dataset and retrain Diffusion Policy with temporal ensembling.
  • Evaluate additional instrument geometries including curved, hinged, and non-parallel designs.
  • Apply domain randomization (textures, lighting, camera pose, friction, mass) to reduce the sim-to-real gap.
  • Add recovery behaviors after detection failure, unreachable grasp, or pick-lift failure.
  • Transfer and validate the full pipeline on a real Franka Emika Panda robot.

10.3 Conclusion

Restate the problem, summarise the shared environment and the hybrid RF-DETR-Seg + FSM + Diffusion Policy system, explain why concentrating learned behaviour on the contact-rich local phase was the key design decision, report the most important measured findings, and state what remains to be done.
